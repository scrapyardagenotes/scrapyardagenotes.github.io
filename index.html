How Much Fabric for a Standard Pillowcase

Cutting a pillowcase from a yard of fabric looks simple until the folds and seams eat into the length. A Standard pillow that finishes around 20 by 26 inches needs more than a quick guess if you want a neat envelope or housewife style case. These notes collect the cut sizes I use at the table so I stop shorting the cuff.

Start with the finished size

A Standard pillowcase usually finishes near 20 by 26 inches. Queen is closer to 20 by 30, and King to 20 by 36. Measure the pillow you actually own. Stuffing loft and a thick insert change how much fabric you need by an inch or two. Write the finished width and length on a scrap before you touch the rotary cutter.

Body and cuff math

For a classic housewife case with a cuff, cut the body a little longer than the finished length so you can turn the hem, and cut a separate cuff strip across the full width of the fabric. Many makers cut the body about 21 by 32 inches for a Standard case from 42 inch wide quilting cotton, then add a cuff strip about 9 to 11 inches deep, folded in half so the raw edges sit in the seam.

Envelope style needs a longer flap so the pillow stays covered when you toss the bed. Add the flap length to the body before you cut. A flap of 6 to 8 inches is common for Standard cases. If the print is directional, cut both pieces with the motif running the same way so the cuff and body match when they meet.

Seam allowance and turn of cloth

A quarter inch seam is fine for a pillowcase, but the turn of cloth at the cuff and the hem still eats length. If you skimp on the body, the finished case looks tight on the pillow. Press the cuff seam toward the body, then topstitch if you want a flat finish that survives the wash.

Why a chart helps

Bolt width, seam allowance, and whether the fabric is directional all shift the numbers. Wideback fabric changes the story again because you can sometimes cut the body across the width without piecing. I keep a free pillowcase yardage chart open when I cut so I do not short the cuff. Scrap friendly tips

Leftover borders from a lap quilt often make a good cuff. Pair a plain body with a pieced cuff if you want a scrap look without piecing the whole case. Prewash both fabrics together if one is a darker print so they shrink the same way.

Finishing notes

Trim threads, press the case flat, and try it on the pillow before you gift it. If the cuff sits short, lengthen the next cuff strip by an inch and keep the same body cut. A small notebook of cut sizes for Standard, Queen and King saves time on the next batch.

Cutting order I use

Wash and press the fabric first. Cut the cuff strip across the width, then cut the body from the remaining length. Mark the fold line on the cuff with a light crease so the fold lands in the middle of the strip. Pin the cuff to one short end of the body, right sides together, sew the seam, press, then fold the cuff to the inside and stitch it down close to the seam.

Close the side seams next, matching the cuff seam so both sides look even. Turn the case right side out and press again. A short stay stitch at the cuff corners keeps the fold from rolling out in the wash.
